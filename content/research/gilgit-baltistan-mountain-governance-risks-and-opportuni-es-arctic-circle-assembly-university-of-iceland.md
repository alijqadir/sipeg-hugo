**Introduction:**

***Northern Areas of Pakistan, renamed in 2009 as Gilgit-Baltistan (GB), situated at the confluence***

***of the Karakoram, Himalayas and Hindukush mountain ranges, has historically been defined by***

***remoteness, difficult terrain and strategic location. The construction of the Karakoram Highway***

***(KKH), followed by expanded road networks, tunnels, aviation, telecommunications and the***

***China–Pakistan Economic Corridor (CPEC), has progressively transformed this relationship***

***between geography, state and society. The region’s political economy is undergoing***

***transformation; once a remote frontier is increasingly becoming a corridor of connectivity,***

***tourism, trade, resource extraction and geopolitical competition. This transformation can be***

***understood as a process of “taming” the rugged mountain region, not through the conquest of***

***nature, but through infrastructure, technology and institutions that weaken the constraints imposed***

***by distance and topography. Concurrently, this process has generated an equally important new***

***challenge: Climate change, glacier dynamics, landslides, flash floods and seismic risks***

***increasingly threaten the infrastructure that has made mountain integration possible.***

***The policy challenge is; transiting from connecting the mountains to governing mountain risk.***

***Tourism, mineral development and digital connectivity offer new opportunities for livelihood***

***diversification, but they also raise questions of ecological sustainability, community ownership***

***and fair distribution of benefits. The future governance strategy in GB, therefore, needs to combine***

***connectivity with resilience, economic integration with environmental stewardship, adaptive***

***management, and technological innovation with community participation. To understand***

***dynamics of this socio-economic and geo-strategic change I will briefly analyze seven key factors***
