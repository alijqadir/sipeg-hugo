Samuel P. Huntington (1927-2008), an influential American scholar and strategic thinker, advanced a provocative thesis in his widely discussed article, “*The Clash of Civilizations*?”, published in **Foreign Affairs (September 1993).** In it, he hypothesized a potential alignment between Confucian and Islamic states (think Gulf states, Pakistan and beyond) and prescribed how the West might counter such a development. Huntington clarified that the emerging global tensions were not necessarily a “clash of civilizations” per se, but rather a conflict in which civilizations functioned as identity markers. In this framework, non-Western societies (West verses the Rest) could evolve into strategic competitors or challengers to the West and would therefore necessitate curbing.

Huntington demonstrated foresight in arguing that China was not only advancing economically and expanding its diplomatic footprint, but was also emerging as a challenger to U.S. military power and global predominance. Crucially, he located China’s rise within a civilizational context; one grounded in values and cultural traditions different from those of the West. He observed, “*a central focus of conflict for the immediate future will be between the West and several Islamic–Confucian states.”* He conceptualized this conflict primarily in terms of arms competition, including nuclear capabilities, missile development, and the transfer of advanced weapons and military technologies. Accordingly, Huntington argued that Western strategy must operate on both short-term and long-term goals. In the short term, while strengthening alliances across Europe; both Eastern and Western, including Russia, he offered a meticulous prescription:

“***Incorporate into the West societies in Eastern Europe and Latin America whose cultures are close to those of the West; promote and maintain cooperative relations with Russia and Japan; prevent the escalation of local inter-civilization conflicts into major inter-civilization wars; limit the expansion of the military strength of Confucian and Islamic states; moderate the reduction of Western military capabilities and maintain military superiority in East and Southwest Asia; exploit differences and conflicts among Confucian and Islamic states; support in other civilizations groups sympathetic to Western values and interests; strengthen international institutions that reflect and legitimate Western interests and values; and promote the involvement of non-Western states in those institutions.”***

From the signing of the Abraham Accords in September 2020 to the live Israel–U.S. military attack against Iran (February 2026, ongoing), an important question arises: are these developments merely tactical responses aimed at containing or punishing Iran, or do they reflect a broader U.S. strategic effort to pre-empt or disrupt a perceived Confucian–Islamic convergence? I don’t know the answer but I wonder if the Chinese academics and policy makers or you perceive this convergence limited to energy security, supply chains and safe guarding investments or is it much more and beyond?

This question invites a deeper reflection on whether Huntington’s civilizational framing continues to inform strategic thinking in Washington, and whether contemporary alignments in the Middle East and beyond are shaped by the logic of preventing such cross-civilizational collaboration.
